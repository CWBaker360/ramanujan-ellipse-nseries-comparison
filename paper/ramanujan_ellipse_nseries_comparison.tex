\begin{abstract}
Ramanujan's formulas for $\pi$ exhibit extraordinary acceleration through
modular and hypergeometric structure. The N-Series framework approaches the
same constant from a different direction: it begins with constructible
polygonal approximations to the circle and accelerates them by cancelling
successive even-power error terms. This note compares these two viewpoints.
Ramanujan-type formulas reveal a deep analytic acceleration mechanism, while
the N-Series construction defines a geometric limiting constant, denoted
$\pi_g$, through chordal polygonal refinement. The comparison is
complementary rather than competitive: Ramanujan's formulas give exceptional
closed-form series acceleration, while the N-Series gives a constructive
geometric cancellation pathway to $\pi$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Exact Ellipse Perimeter}

For an ellipse with semi-major axis $a$ and semi-minor axis $b$, the exact
perimeter is expressed in terms of the complete elliptic integral of the
second kind:
\[
  P_{\mathrm{exact}} = 4a\,E(e),
  \qquad
  e = \sqrt{1-\frac{b^{2}}{a^{2}}},
\]
where
\[
  E(e) = \int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1-e^{2}\sin^{2}t}\,\mathrm{d}t .
\]
This exact expression is not elementary in general. For this reason, the
ellipse perimeter has long served as a classical testing ground for
geometric, algebraic, and numerical approximation methods.

\section{Ramanujan's Approximation}

Ramanujan gave several remarkably accurate closed-form approximations for the
ellipse perimeter. One of the most widely used is
\[
  P_{\mathrm{R}}
  = \pi(a+b)\left[\,1 + \frac{3h}{10+\sqrt{4-3h}}\,\right],
  \qquad
  h = \left(\frac{a-b}{a+b}\right)^{2}.
\]
This formula is exceptional because it compresses elliptic-integral behavior
into a simple algebraic expression involving $a$, $b$, and $h$. Its strength
is that it gives a high-accuracy perimeter estimate without requiring an
explicit refinement process.

\section{Polygonal Approximation and N-Series Acceleration}

The N-Series viewpoint approaches the same problem from the opposite
direction. Instead of seeking a single closed-form approximation, one begins
with a geometric polygonal approximation to the ellipse and then accelerates
the convergence by cancelling leading error terms.

Let $P_N$ denote the perimeter of the inscribed $N$-segment polygon obtained
by sampling the ellipse
\[
  x(t)=a\cos t, \qquad y(t)=b\sin t, \qquad 0\leq t<2\pi,
\]
at equally spaced parameter values
\[
  t_j=\frac{2\pi j}{N}, \qquad j=0,1,\ldots,N-1 .
\]
Then
\[
  P_N = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
  \sqrt{\bigl(x(t_{j+1})-x(t_j)\bigr)^2 + \bigl(y(t_{j+1})-y(t_j)\bigr)^2},
\]
with $t_N=t_0+2\pi$.

For smooth closed curves, such symmetric polygonal approximations possess an
even-power asymptotic expansion of the form
\[
  P_N = P_{\mathrm{exact}}
  + \frac{C_2}{N^2} + \frac{C_4}{N^4} + \frac{C_6}{N^6} + \cdots .
\]
This is precisely the structure targeted by the N-Series cancellation
operators.

The two-scale accelerated perimeter is
\[
  \widehat{P}^{(2)}_N = P_{2N} + \frac{P_{2N}-P_N}{2^2-1}.
\]
If the leading error term is $C_2 N^{-2}$, this operator cancels it exactly
and gives
\[
  \widehat{P}^{(2)}_N = P_{\mathrm{exact}} + O(N^{-4}).
\]

Using the three scales $N$, $2N$, and $4N$, one may cancel the $N^{-2}$ and
$N^{-4}$ terms simultaneously. The resulting three-scale operator is
\[
  \widehat{P}^{(3)}_N
  = \frac{1}{45}\,P_N - \frac{4}{9}\,P_{2N} + \frac{64}{45}\,P_{4N}.
\]
(Its three weights sum to $1$, and they are fixed by the requirement that the
$N^{-2}$ and $N^{-4}$ terms vanish; one checks
$\tfrac{1}{45}-\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot\tfrac{4}{9}+\tfrac{1}{16}\cdot\tfrac{64}{45}=0$
and the analogous $N^{-4}$ condition.) This gives
\[
  \widehat{P}^{(3)}_N = P_{\mathrm{exact}} + O(N^{-6}),
\]
provided the even-power expansion holds.

\section{Numerical Comparison}

We tested ellipses with $a=1$ and
\[
  b \in \{0.90,\;0.75,\;0.50,\;0.25\}.
\]
For each ellipse, the exact perimeter was computed from the elliptic-integral
formula and compared against Ramanujan's approximation, the raw polygonal
perimeter $P_N$, the two-scale N-Series approximation of order $O(N^{-4})$,
and the three-scale N-Series approximation of order $O(N^{-6})$.

The results show two complementary regimes. For the near-circular ellipse
$a=1$, $b=0.90$, Ramanujan's formula is exceptionally accurate, with absolute
error approximately $2.24\times 10^{-17}$. At $N=256$, the three-scale
N-Series approximation reaches error approximately $6.32\times 10^{-17}$,
which is comparable but does not yet improve on Ramanujan's value.

For more eccentric ellipses, however, the N-Series ladder becomes dominant. At
$a=1$, $b=0.75$, Ramanujan's error is approximately $4.61\times 10^{-13}$. The
three-scale N-Series approximation improves on this already at $N=64$, where
the error is approximately $2.40\times 10^{-13}$, and by $N=256$ reduces the
error to approximately $5.85\times 10^{-17}$.

The improvement is more pronounced for $a=1$, $b=0.50$. Here Ramanujan's error
is approximately $2.21\times 10^{-9}$, whereas the three-scale N-Series
approximation at $N=32$ already gives error approximately $7.34\times
10^{-12}$. By $N=256$, the three-scale N-Series error is approximately
$5.13\times 10^{-17}$.

For the more eccentric case $a=1$, $b=0.25$, Ramanujan's error is
approximately $1.07\times 10^{-6}$. The three-scale N-Series approximation
improves on this even at $N=32$, with error approximately $1.81\times
10^{-7}$, and reaches approximately $4.54\times 10^{-17}$ by $N=256$.

The three-scale error approximately falls by a factor of $64$ per scale
doubling --- for $b=0.50$, the successive values are
$2.10\times 10^{-13}$ at $N=64$, $3.28\times 10^{-15}$ at $N=128$, and
$5.13\times 10^{-17}$ at $N=256$ --- in agreement with the predicted
$O(N^{-6})$ order.

\section{Interpretation}

The comparison does not show a single universal winner. Rather, it shows two
different acceleration mechanisms.

Ramanujan's formula gives an exceptional one-shot algebraic approximation,
especially near the circular case. The N-Series method gives a systematic
geometric refinement ladder whose advantage increases as the ellipse becomes
more eccentric. In this sense, the N-Series does not replace Ramanujan's
approximation; it complements it by supplying a constructible,
scale-cancellation pathway for driving the polygonal ellipse perimeter to
high precision.

This distinction is important. Ramanujan's approximation is closed-form and
requires no refinement parameter $N$. The N-Series approximation is
algorithmic: it begins with chordal polygon approximations and then cancels
the leading even-power error terms. Thus the two methods illuminate different
structures in the same classical problem. Ramanujan reveals a hidden algebraic
approximation to the elliptic integral, while the N-Series reveals a
scale-cancellation structure inside the polygonal geometry of the ellipse.


\section{Conclusion}

The ellipse perimeter problem provides a useful bridge between Ramanujan-type
approximation and N-Series geometric acceleration. Ramanujan's approximation
is extraordinarily effective near the circular case and remains impressive
even for significant eccentricity. The N-Series refinement ladder, however,
systematically improves with scale and overtakes the Ramanujan approximation
for the tested moderate and high eccentricities.

The conclusion is therefore complementary rather than competitive:
Ramanujan's formula supplies a powerful closed-form algebraic estimate, while
the N-Series supplies a systematic geometric pathway to high precision
through exact cancellation of even-power polygonal error terms.
